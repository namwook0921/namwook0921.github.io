Why they look like different people: a camera projects the world through a single point, and an object's size in the image falls off with its distance from that point. What distorts a face is therefore not the absolute distance but the ratio of distances between its near and far parts.

At 1.5 ft my nose is about 1.3 ft from the lens while my ears are about 1.8 ft away — a ratio of roughly 1.4×. The nose gets projected some 40% larger than it should be relative to the ears, which is the bulge in the left photo. The lenses pin down the second distance for me: 24 mm → 151 mm is a 6.3× magnification, so to keep my face the same size in the frame I had to stand about 6.3× farther back — call it 9.5 ft. Now the nose sits at 9.3 ft and the ears at 9.8 ft, a ratio of 1.05×. The relative magnification nearly vanishes and the face flattens into the proportions we read as accurate.

Worth saying clearly: zooming in fixed nothing by itself. It only bought back the framing I gave up by walking backwards. The lens sets the field of view, my feet set the perspective. That is why portrait photographers reach for 85–135 mm — those focal lengths force them to stand far enough away that the face stops distorting.

Same ratio argument as the selfie, scaled up from inches to a whole building. Top row is Galvez Hall. Going from 24 mm to 63 mm is a 2.6× magnification, so keeping the building the same size in the frame meant standing 2.6× farther away — and stepping back adds the same distance to every part of the arcade, not the same proportion.

Estimating: in the close shot the nearest arch is maybe 25 ft away and the far end of the arcade 100 ft — a ratio of 4×, which is why the far arches look tiny and the roofline dives. Walking back 40 ft makes those 65 ft and 140 ft: a ratio of 2.2×. The far end is now rendered about half the size of the near end instead of a quarter, so the arcade reads almost like an elevation drawing.

Bottom row is Hoover Tower, and it shows the same effect running vertically. The tower is about 285 ft tall, so standing 40 ft from the base puts the dome roughly 290 ft from the lens — a 7× ratio between base and top, which is the hard taper in the left frame.

The step back here was smaller (60 mm → 77 mm, only 1.3×), so the effect is gentler, but it points the same way: moving to about 50 ft from the base barely changes the distance to the dome, which is dominated by height, so the ratio falls to roughly 5.7×. The taper eases and the dome grows relative to the shaft. To flatten it properly I'd need to be a few hundred feet out with a much longer lens.

Part 3 · Dolly zoom

The olives stay exactly where they are. The kitchen panics. 🫒

83 1 comment
Namwook Lee Author

This one puts the first two posts together. Zooming in scales everything in the frame by the same factor, because it only narrows the field of view. Walking backwards scales things by different factors, because it adds the same absolute distance to everything — which is a big fractional change for a jar 3 ft away and almost nothing for a wall 15 ft behind it.

The dolly zoom cancels one against the other, but only for the subject: at each step the zoom is picked to exactly undo the shrinking caused by the step backwards, so the jar is pinned. The background gets that same zoom and almost none of the distance penalty, so it is left with a net magnification and appears to swell. Walk in while zooming out and it collapses instead.

What is really changing is the field of view around a fixed subject size — the perspective slides continuously. It feels wrong precisely because our visual system reads a fixed subject as a fixed viewpoint, and here the viewpoint never stops moving.

Wrap-up

Three posts, one fact: perspective is set by where the camera stands, and focal length is only a crop. "Wide-angle distortion" and "telephoto compression" are both really distance effects that get blamed on lenses, because we habitually pair short lenses with close positions and long ones with far ones. What I'll actually keep: pick where to stand first, based on the depth relationships I want, then pick a focal length to frame it.

The One Idea

Perspective is set by where you stand. Focal length only decides how much of the world you keep.

Overview

Three small photographic experiments about one idea: what a photo looks like is decided less by the lens than by where the camera is standing. A zoom changes how much of the world lands in the frame; it does not change the geometry of what is already there.

All of it was shot on an iPhone 17 Pro, which makes the experiment easy to run — switching between the 24 mm main camera and the 151 mm telephoto is the same as swapping lenses, and the EXIF data records exactly which one took each frame.

Part 1: Selfie — The Wrong Way and The Right Way 2 photos

The first photo is taken the way most selfies are taken: arm's length, about a foot and a half from my face, on the wide main camera. For the second I stepped back and switched to the telephoto, zooming until my face filled the frame by the same amount. Ten seconds apart, same light, same face — only the distance changed.

Namwook Lee — why this happens

A camera projects the world through a single point, and an object's size in the image falls off with its distance from that point. What causes the distortion is therefore not the absolute distance but the ratio of distances between the near and far parts of the subject.

At 1.5 ft, my nose is about 1.3 ft from the lens while my ears are about 1.8 ft away — a ratio of roughly 1.4×. The nose is projected some 40% larger than it should be relative to the ears, which is exactly the bulge in the left photo. The lenses here make the second distance easy to pin down: going from 24 mm to 151 mm is a 6.3× magnification, so to keep my face the same size in the frame I had to stand about 6.3× farther away — call it 9.5 ft. Now the nose sits at 9.3 ft and the ears at 9.8 ft: a ratio of 1.05×. The relative magnification nearly vanishes and the face flattens into the proportions we read as accurate.

Zooming in fixed nothing by itself; it only bought back the framing I gave up by walking backwards. The lens sets the field of view, my feet set the perspective. This is why portrait photographers reach for 85–135 mm lenses — those focal lengths force them to stand far enough away that the face stops distorting.

Part 2: Architectural Perspective Compression 2 photos

I found a facade I could see at an angle across an open space, so that one end of the building was clearly closer to me than the other. I photographed it first from far away, zoomed in; then I walked toward it and shot again at the wide end, stopping when the building filled roughly the same fraction of the frame.

Namwook Lee — why this happens

It is the same ratio argument as the selfie, applied to a subject tens of meters deep instead of a few inches. Say the near corner of the facade is 40 ft away and the far corner 90 ft. Standing close, that is a ratio of 2.25×: the near corner is projected more than twice as large, so the building visibly recedes and its parallel edges converge steeply.

From 200 ft, those same two corners sit at 200 ft and 250 ft — a ratio of 1.25×. The depth of the building is now small compared to how far away all of it is, so every part is magnified by nearly the same amount, the depth cues collapse, and the scene reads like a flat elevation drawing. That is the perspective compression long lenses are famous for: not something the lens does to the light, but a consequence of shooting from far away, with the zoom only making that distant view big enough to fill the frame.

Part 3: The Dolly Zoom animated

The last part puts the first two together. I set up a subject with a background well behind it, then walked backwards while zooming in, taking a still at each step and keeping the subject the same size in every frame. The stills are stitched into the animation below.

Namwook Lee — why this happens

Zooming in scales everything in the frame by the same factor, because it only narrows the field of view. Walking backwards scales things by different factors, because it changes every object's distance by the same absolute amount but by very different proportions — one step back is a large fractional change for a subject 3 ft away and a negligible one for a wall 60 ft away.

The dolly zoom cancels one against the other, but only for the subject. At each step the zoom is chosen to exactly undo the shrinking caused by the step backwards, so the subject is pinned in place. The background gets the same zoom and almost none of the distance penalty, so it is left with a net magnification and appears to swell. Run it the other way — walk in while zooming out — and the background collapses away instead.

What is really changing is the field of view around a fixed subject size, which is another way of saying the perspective is sliding continuously. The shot is unsettling precisely because our visual system reads a fixed subject as a fixed viewpoint, and here the viewpoint never stops moving.

What I Took Away

All three parts are the same fact seen from different angles: perspective is set by the position of the center of projection, and focal length is only a crop. "Wide-angle distortion" and "telephoto compression" are both really distance effects that get blamed on lenses, because we habitually pair short lenses with close positions and long ones with far positions.

The practical version I'll keep: decide where to stand first, based on the depth relationships I want, then pick a focal length to frame it.

Project 0

Becoming Friends with Your Camera

Three small photographic experiments about one idea: what an image looks like is decided less by the lens than by where you stand. Focal length only sets how much of the world you crop.

Part 1

Selfie: the wrong way and the right way

Same face, same framing, two very different noses.

The first photo is taken the way most selfies are taken: arm's length, roughly a foot and a half from my face. For the second, I stepped back several feet and zoomed in until the face filled the frame by the same amount. Nothing about the subject changed — only the distance from the camera.

Why this happens

A camera projects the world through a single point, and an object's size in the image falls off with its distance from that point. What matters for distortion is therefore not the absolute distance but the ratio of distances between the near and far parts of the subject.

At 1.5 ft, my nose is roughly 1.3 ft away while my ears are roughly 1.8 ft away — a ratio of about 1.4×. The nose is projected about 40% larger than it "should" be relative to the ears, which is exactly the bulbous look in the left photo. Step back to 8 ft and those same two features sit at about 7.8 ft and 8.3 ft — a ratio of roughly 1.06×. The relative magnification nearly vanishes, so the face flattens into the proportions we recognize as accurate.

Zooming in did not fix anything by itself; it only restored the framing I gave up by walking backwards. The lens changes the field of view, the feet change the perspective. This is why portrait photographers reach for 85–135 mm lenses: those focal lengths force them to stand far enough away that the face stops distorting.

Part 2

Architectural perspective compression

A building photographed twice, from far and near, at the same apparent size.

I found a facade I could view at an angle across an open space, so that one end of the building was clearly closer to me than the other. I photographed it first from far away, zoomed in; then I walked toward it and shot again at the wide end, stopping when the building filled roughly the same fraction of the frame.

Why this happens

It is the same ratio argument as the selfie, applied to a subject tens of meters deep instead of a few inches. Say the near corner of the facade is 40 ft away and the far corner 90 ft. Standing close, that is a ratio of 2.25×: the near corner is projected more than twice as large, so the building visibly recedes and the parallel edges converge steeply.

From 200 ft, the same two corners sit at 200 ft and 250 ft — a ratio of 1.25×. The depth of the building is small compared to how far away all of it is, so every part is magnified by nearly the same amount. Depth cues collapse and the scene reads as a flat elevation drawing. This is the perspective compression that long lenses are known for: it is not something the lens does to the light, it is a consequence of shooting from far away, and the zoom merely makes that distant view large enough to fill the frame.

Same effect, different name

Telephoto shots of a crowded street where the people look stacked on top of each other, and wide-angle real-estate photos where a small room looks cavernous, are the two ends of this one effect.

Part 3

The dolly zoom

Hold the subject still; let the world behind it breathe.

The last part puts the previous two together. I set up a subject with a background well behind it, then walked backwards while zooming in, taking a still at each step and keeping the subject the same size in every frame. The frames are stitched into the animation below.

Why this happens

Zooming in scales everything in the frame by the same factor, because it only narrows the field of view. Walking backwards scales things by different factors, because it changes each object's distance by the same absolute amount but by very different proportions — a step back is a large fractional change for a subject 3 ft away and a negligible one for a wall 60 ft away.

The dolly zoom cancels one against the other for the subject only. The zoom is chosen at each step to exactly undo the shrinking caused by the step backwards, so the subject is pinned. The background gets the same zoom but almost none of the distance penalty, so it is left with a net magnification and appears to swell. Run it the other way — walk in while zooming out — and the background collapses away instead.

What is actually changing is the field of view around a fixed subject size, which is another way of saying the perspective is changing continuously. The shot is unsettling precisely because our visual system reads a fixed subject as a fixed viewpoint, and here the viewpoint is sliding the whole time.

Wrap-up

What I took away

All three parts are the same fact seen from different angles: perspective is set by the position of the center of projection, and focal length is only a crop. "Wide-angle distortion" and "telephoto compression" are both really distance effects that get blamed on lenses because we habitually pair short lenses with close positions and long lenses with far ones.

The practical version I'll keep: decide where to stand first, based on the depth relationships I want, then pick a focal length to frame it.
